Build a world, release life, and see how long it lasts.

Nothing here is told what to become. Organisms are bodies of cells that must pay for themselves in energy. They eat, move, breed with small copying errors, and die. Plants, grazers, hunters and scavengers all start simple. Whatever survives is what evolution found. Your job is the stage: how big, how harsh, and who arrives.

Founders

Every founder of a group shares one simple body. Grazers have a mouth, a mover and an eye; predators add a killer cell; scavengers digest dead matter.

Climate and evolution

How the world works
  1. Bodies are made of cells. Producers turn light into energy. Mouths eat. Movers move. Killers wound other bodies. Armour blocks killers and keeps out the cold. Eyes see in one direction.
  2. Everything costs energy. Each cell has upkeep, so a part only spreads if it pays for itself. Upkeep scales like real metabolism: a body twice the size costs less than twice as much.
  3. Breeding copies the body with mistakes. A child can gain a random cell, lose one, duplicate one, or change one. Diet, how much to save before breeding, how to react to what the eyes see, and the mutation rate itself all drift too.
  4. Diet is a single sliding gene from living plants, through dead matter, to meat. Specialists digest their food best, but a little omnivory costs little, so lineages can drift from one way of life to another.
  5. Killer cells cut both ways. Prey that carry them strike back when attacked, so a killer cell can pay for itself as defence long before a lineage eats meat.
  6. Grazing wears plants down. A lightly grazed plant regrows from its rootstock, but heavy, sustained grazing kills it. Dead plants leave seeds in the soil that sprout once the ground is bare again, so a crash is followed by regrowth.
  7. Plants compete for light and fight back. Bigger plants shade smaller neighbours, and armour cells make a plant harder to eat. Both cost energy, so small, fast-spreading plants and big or armoured ones each win in different places and times.
  8. Doing everything costs extra. A body with both producer cells and a mouth runs two systems, so it is 20% worse at each than a specialist.
  9. Crowded species catch more disease. Once a species packs more than about 2% of the ground, extra deaths set in, so no single strategy can take over for good. Rare species, like most predators, escape it.
  10. Plants need soil as well as light. Bogs are bright but starved of nutrients. Meat is rich in them, so a plant that catches animals can grow there.
  11. Nobody is scored. There is no fitness function. The species list, the names and the roles are labels added afterwards for you to read.

Where the rules come from

  • Metabolic scaling. Upkeep grows with body size to the ¾ power, and lifespan with the ¼ power. Brown et al. 2004, Toward a metabolic theory of ecology
  • Energy is lost at every step up the food chain, which is why predators are always rarer than their prey. Lindeman 1942, The trophic-dynamic aspect of ecology
  • Space, refuges and arrival order keep predators and prey together. Forests hide prey and slow hunters; succession establishes prey before predators arrive. Huffaker 1958, mite and orange experiments
  • Crowded hunters get in each other's way, which damps boom and bust cycles. van Voorn et al. 2008, Beddington–DeAngelis predator interference
  • More food can mean less stability. Turning sunlight up can make cycles swing wider, not calmer. Rosenzweig 1971, the paradox of enrichment
  • Carnivorous plants pay off in sunny, nutrient-poor places, where light is cheap and nitrogen is scarce. That is why the bog exists. Givnish et al., the cost-benefit model of carnivory (1984, revisited 2018)
  • Mixotrophs pay for flexibility. Organisms that both photosynthesise and eat must maintain two sets of machinery, and that cost is what stops them beating specialists everywhere. Ward et al. 2011; they still win where resources are scarce, Tittel et al. 2003
  • Animals leave a patch when it stops paying. They stop to eat only while intake beats their running costs. Charnov 1976, the marginal value theorem.
  • Complex parts are built from simpler steps, so new cells are added one at a time and must each earn their keep. Lenski et al. 2003, The evolutionary origin of complex features
  • Species are drawn by genetic distance from the lineage's founder, as in NEAT. Stanley & Miikkulainen 2002
  • Ongoing novelty needs a world that keeps changing, mostly because the other organisms keep evolving. That is why the food web, seasons and disasters are here. Taylor 2015, Requirements for open-ended evolution
